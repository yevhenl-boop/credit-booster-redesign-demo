Credit Complaints Rose 17.7x Since 2020. Phone Complaints Rose 1.06x.

Credit reporting complaints to the CFPB exploded from 283,716 in 2020 to 4,810,498 in 2025. Complaints filed by telephone went from 5,232 to 5,554. The entire increase arrived through a web form.

Alexander Katsman

July 16, 2026

Everyone reported the explosion. Nobody asked how it arrived.

The headline number is public and it’s been quoted everywhere. The CFPB’s own December 2025 report leads with “an almost 3,000% increase in NCRA complaints since January 1, 2020.”

True. And close to meaningless on its own.

Because here’s what happens when you split those complaints by how they were filed. Credit complaints filed on the web rose 17.7x between 2020 and 2025, from 271,010 to 4,799,271. Complaints filed by telephone over the identical period rose 1.06x, from 5,232 to 5,554.

Not 106%. 1.06 times. 322 more phone calls in five years.

The entire explosion is web. Every additional complaint arrived through a form.

What we measured

The CFPB records how each complaint reached it, in a field called Submitted via. We counted credit reporting complaints by that field, per year, and indexed to 2020 because 2020 is the CFPB’s own baseline in the report we’re extending. Not because it flatters the trend.

The channel nobody checked

Phone is an accidental control group, and it’s the most useful thing in this dataset.

Filing by phone means a human being calls the CFPB and talks to a person. You can’t bulk-submit it. You can’t script it. It’s slow and it’s annoying, which is exactly what makes it a clean measurement of one thing: how many people are distressed enough to pick up the phone.

That number has not moved in six years. 5,232 in 2020. 5,554 in 2025. Postal mail, same story, flat.

So run the logic. If a wave of genuine new credit trouble had hit American files after 2020, it should show up in every channel. Distressed people call. They write. They didn’t.

What this rules in. This is strong evidence that the surge is a change in how complaints are submitted, not purely a change in the underlying volume of credit problems. It’s consistent with automation, bulk submission, and third-party filing at scale.

What it does not prove, and we’re not going to let this slide past: it does not prove any particular actor caused it, and it does not prove that no real increase in credit problems happened. Web filing also grew because filing online got easier, better known, and more heavily marketed. Both things can be true. We’re not picking one and dressing it up as a finding.

Sourced rather than speculated, here’s the regulator’s own read. The CFPB attributes complaint growth partly to “third parties using the complaint process, including credit repair organizations and those who offer credit advice online and on social media platforms (so called ‘finfluencers’ or ‘FinTok’)” and to “an increase in the number of complaints submitted in a single session” (CFPB, FCRA 611(e) report, December 2025). That’s their assessment. Our channel data is consistent with it. It isn’t proof of it.

The caveat that belongs on every complaint statistic, including ours

This is the part that costs us, so we’ll say it plainly.

Every other page we publish from this dataset counts complaints. 1,923,820 “not mine” complaints. 383,484 complaints in Georgia. 4,810,498 credit complaints in 2025. All real counts. All from this file.

And every one of them is a count of complaints filed, not of people harmed or errors confirmed. The channel split is the clearest possible evidence of why those came apart after 2020. If a number in this dataset went up 17.7x while the hardest-to-automate way of producing it went up 1.06x, then the number is measuring submission behavior at least as much as it’s measuring credit problems.

So if you’re quoting a CFPB complaint volume, quote this one next to it. Including ours.

What this means for your file

Honestly? It means be skeptical of anyone waving a complaint count at you, and that includes us.

There’s a practical read, though. A complaint is not a fix. Nearly five million credit complaints got filed in 2025 and the top one was “this isn’t mine,” which means a lot of people are using a federal complaint form as their first move.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much did credit complaints actually increase?

Credit reporting complaints to the CFPB went from 283,716 in 2020 to 4,810,498 in 2025, which is 17.0x on the yearly totals. Web-filed credit complaints specifically went from 271,010 to 4,799,271, which is 17.7x. The CFPB's own December 2025 report describes an almost 3,000% increase in complaints about the three big bureaus since January 1, 2020.

Why do phone complaints matter here?

Because filing by phone requires a person to call the CFPB and talk to someone, which is hard to automate or do in bulk. It works as an accidental control group. It went from 5,232 in 2020 to 5,554 in 2025, essentially flat, while web filings went up 17.7x.

Does this prove the complaints are fake?

No, and we are not claiming that. It is strong evidence that the explosion is a change in how complaints get submitted rather than purely a change in how many credit problems exist. Web filing also grew because filing online got easier and better known. Both can be true at once.

What share of credit complaints are filed online?

99.77% of credit reporting complaints in 2025 arrived through the web. Phone was about 0.12% and postal mail about 0.08%. Fax reached zero by 2023.

Should I trust CFPB complaint statistics at all?

Yes, as a measure of complaint volume, which is what they are. The mistake is reading them as a measure of consumer harm. A count of complaints filed is not a count of people harmed or errors confirmed, and the channel split is the clearest evidence of why those two things came apart after 2020.
