Tradelines for Your Credit Score: The Word Hides Three Different Things

A tradeline is simply an account on your credit report. Every card, loan, and mortgage is one. But when someone sells “tradelines for your credit score,” they mean something narrower: getting an account with someone else’s good history added to your file. Whether that helps, does nothing, or lands you in a fraud file depends entirely on which of three versions you are looking at.

The three versions, honestly labeled:

  1. The free authorized-user add. A family member or friend puts you on a card they already pay on time. Legal, free, and the one version that reliably belongs in a credit plan.
  2. The purchased authorized-user spot. A broker rents you onto a stranger’s aged card. Legal gray zone, violates card issuer agreements, and increasingly discounted by lenders.
  3. The “primary tradeline” for sale. A fake or fraudulent account in your own name, often bundled with a CPN. Not a gray zone: this is the version prosecuted as bank fraud, with penalties up to 30 years.

This page walks all three. The deep dives are is buying tradelines legal for version two and the primary tradelines warning for version three.

How a Tradeline Moves a Score, When It Does

A FICO score weighs payment history at 35% and amounts owed at 30%. An authorized-user tradeline works by importing three things from the primary holder’s account into your file: their on-time payment record, their available credit, and the account’s age.

That is why the same tradeline does different things on different files. On a thin file with two young accounts, an aged card with a high limit and spotless history can matter. On a file already carrying real negatives, it papers over nothing: the late payments and collections that are dragging you down are still there, still weighted at 35%. Experian’s guidance adds the timing detail: the account typically appears within a month or two of the issuer being notified.

The honest caveats travel together. The boost lasts only while you stay on the account, the primary holder can remove you at any time, and an underwriter who sees one aged account that does not fit the rest of your file can simply discount it.

Authorized User vs Primary: The Line That Matters

Authorized user means your name rides on someone else’s account. You get the history, you are not liable for the debt, and the arrangement is legal. The full mechanics are in the authorized user tradelines guide.

Primary means the account is yours: you applied, you owe, you pay. Real primary tradelines are the strongest thing on any credit report, and there is exactly one legitimate way to get one: open it yourself. A secured card or a credit builder loan is a real primary tradeline that costs little and reports for years.

Anyone selling you a “seasoned primary tradeline” is selling a contradiction. You cannot buy a years-old account that was legitimately yours the whole time. What is actually delivered is fabricated account history or a synthetic identity built on a CPN, and federal prosecutors treat both as bank fraud. Bureau defenses are tightening around the whole category too: in January 2026 Equifax launched its Credit Abuse Risk model targeting first-party fraud patterns like credit washing, working alongside its synthetic identity detection tools.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a tradeline on a credit report?

Every account on your credit report is a tradeline: each credit card, loan, and mortgage, with its payment history, balance, and age. When sellers advertise tradelines for your credit score, they almost always mean paid authorized-user spots on someone else’s aged credit card, which is one narrow and risky slice of what the word actually covers.

Do tradelines really raise your credit score?

A well-chosen authorized-user tradeline can help, most visibly on a thin or young file, because it imports another account’s on-time history and available credit into your report. The honest caveats: the boost varies, lenders discount authorized-user accounts that do not fit the rest of a file, the primary holder can remove you at any time, and it fixes nothing that is actually wrong on your report.

How much does a tradeline cost?

The legal version is free: a family member or friend adds you to a card they already manage well. Brokers charge real money for stranger tradelines, with prices that scale with the account’s age and limit, and that purchased version is the one that violates card issuer agreements and carries the risk. Inside Credit Booster AI’s marketplace, each tradeline shows its price up front before you decide.

Are tradelines legal?

Being an authorized user is legal. Buying a spot on a stranger’s card through a broker is a gray area: it violates card issuer agreements, and using the inflated score to mislead a lender can cross into fraud. So-called primary tradelines sold for cash, and any package involving a CPN, sit in outright fraud territory prosecuted under federal bank fraud law.

How long does a tradeline take to show up?

Experian’s guidance is that an authorized-user account typically appears on your reports within a month or two of being added. It helps only while it reports, and if the primary holder removes you or the card issuer closes the account, the history leaves your file with it.

What is better than buying a tradeline?

History you own. A free family authorized-user add, a secured card, or a credit builder loan all report durable on-time payments in your name, cannot be yanked away by a stranger, and never look like a purchased boost to an underwriter. Pair that with clearing genuine report errors, which typically moves a score 30 to 60 points when real errors exist.
