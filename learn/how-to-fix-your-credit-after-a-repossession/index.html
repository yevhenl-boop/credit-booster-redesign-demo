How to Fix Your Credit After a Repossession

A repossession stays on your credit report for seven years. Here is what you can dispute, how the deficiency balance works, and the exact steps to rebuild.

Alexander Katsman

September 12, 2026•11 min read

Last reviewed 12 September 2026.

A repossession stays on your credit report for seven years, and while it is accurate no one can lawfully remove it. What you can remove are the errors around it: a wrong first-delinquency date, a balance that ignores what the car sold for, a deficiency reported twice, a voluntary surrender recorded as a forced repossession.

Repossession was the single most complained-about issue among the roughly 28,500 vehicle loan or lease complaints the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau received in 2025, and that issue ran 124 percent above its prior two-year monthly average (CFPB, 2025 Consumer Response Annual Report, Section 4.7).

That distinction is the whole job. Every step below is one action, with its source.

How long does a repossession stay on your credit report?

A vehicle repossession stays on your credit report for seven years. The Fair Credit Reporting Act at 15 U.S.C. 1681c(a)(5) excludes “any other adverse item of information … which antedates the report by more than seven years.” The same limit applies under 1681c(a)(4) to “accounts placed for collection or charged to profit and loss,” which is how the deficiency is usually reported.

The start date is what most pages get wrong. 15 U.S.C. 1681c(c)(1) starts the period “upon the expiration of the 180-day period beginning on the date of the commencement of the delinquency which immediately preceded the collection activity, charge to profit and loss, or similar action.”

So the clock runs from the first missed payment in the run that ended in the repossession, not the tow date and not the auction date. FICO agrees: “These items will remain on your credit reports for seven years from the date of your first missed payment.”

Lenders do get that date wrong. The CFPB’s 20 August 2014 action against First Investors Financial Services Group, a $2.75 million civil penalty, found it “inaccurately reported many of its customers’ ‘date of first delinquency,’” affecting “tens of thousands of consumers.” Check that field first.

What actually appears on your report after a repossession

A repossession is almost never one line on a credit report. FICO lists the pieces: “Late payments can hurt your FICO Scores significantly,” then “A loan default will be added to your credit reports as a separate event,” then “The lender will report that it has repossessed the collateral,” then a collection account if the deficiency goes unpaid.

One repossessed vehicle can therefore produce three or four separate negative entries, each verified and disputed on its own. Correcting the charged-off auto tradeline does nothing to the collection reporting the same deficiency.

What is a deficiency balance after a repossession?

A deficiency balance is what you still owe after the repossessed car is sold. The FTC defines it precisely on its vehicle repossession page: “The difference between what you owe on your contract (plus certain expenses) and what your lender gets for selling the car is called a deficiency.”

The CFPB puts numbers on it: “if you owe $10,000 on the vehicle and your lender sells it for $7,500, you owe the deficiency of $2,500, plus any other fees for repossession,” and if you do not pay, “the lender is allowed to hire a debt collector to collect it” (Ask CFPB).

This is not rare. In the CFPB’s January 2025 report Repossession in Auto Finance, covering 2018 to 2022, “Of the 905,000 disposals in the dataset, 94 percent ended with a deficiency balance.” Those balances:

Is voluntary surrender better than a repossession?

Voluntary surrender is only slightly better than an involuntary repossession. The FTC is blunt: you “might pay less in fees. But even if you return the car voluntarily, you’re still responsible for paying the difference between what you owe on your contract and what your lender gets for selling the car.”

The CFPB counts voluntary surrender as repossession in its own data. Footnote 16 of Repossession in Auto Finance states: “Unless otherwise noted, we include voluntary surrenders in our analysis of repossession data.” Voluntary repossessions were 15 to 19 percent of all completed repossessions in 2018 and 2019.

Experian says the same: “A repossession, even if it’s voluntary, will affect your credit. It will be noted on your credit report, along with any remaining balance and missed payments, and stay there for seven years from the date you stopped paying your loan.”

One real dispute lives here. If you surrendered the vehicle voluntarily and the tradeline says the lender repossessed it, that is a factual inaccuracy, and the CFPB has enforced on exactly it: the First Investors order found the company “told credit reporting agencies that some of its customers had their vehicles repossessed, when in fact those individuals had voluntarily surrendered their vehicles back to the lienholder.”

How to fix your credit after a repossession, step by step

Step 1. Pull all three credit reports from AnnualCreditReport.com

The FTC confirms weekly access is permanent: all three nationwide credit bureaus “have permanently extended a program that lets you check your credit report from each once a week for free” at AnnualCreditReport.com, or 1-877-322-8228.

Pull all three. Nothing in the FCRA requires a lender to report to every bureau, so a repossession can sit on two reports and not the third, or carry a different date on each one.

Step 2. Demand the lender’s post-sale accounting in writing

What the car sold for sets the deficiency, so you need that number. The FTC notes a public auction may require notice of its timing and location, and a private sale may require disclosure of the sale date.

Ask the lender in writing for the sale date, the gross sale price, every fee charged, and the arithmetic behind the reported balance. If that math does not match your credit report, it is a disputable inaccuracy.

Step 3. Check the repossession entry against six facts

Go line by line on each credit report:

  1. Date of first delinquency, the seven-year anchor under 1681c(c)(1).
  2. Voluntary or involuntary. Does it match what happened?
  3. Balance reported. Does it reflect the auction proceeds or the original loan amount?
  4. Duplicate reporting. Is the same money showing as a charged-off loan and a collection?
  5. Ownership. Is this your account, your signature, your vehicle?
  6. Payment grid. Are the 30, 60 and 90 day marks in the right months?

Step 4. Mail the dispute to each bureau that shows the error

The FTC’s guide to disputing errors on your credit reports says the letter must carry “your complete name and address,” “each mistake that you want fixed, and why,” “copies (not originals) of documents that support your request,” and your report with the mistakes circled.

Send it certified mail, return receipt, and keep the receipt. In the CFPB’s December 2025 report to Congress under FCRA Section 611(e), about 90 percent of complaining consumers said they had already disputed the item, but Equifax confirmed a prior dispute only about 37 percent of the time and TransUnion about 30 percent. Proof of mailing closes that gap.

The FTC states the deadline: “The credit bureau has 30 days to investigate it.” Under 15 U.S.C. 1681i(a)(1)(B) that extends by 15 days if you send more information mid-investigation, and 1681i(a)(6)(A) requires written results “not later than 5 business days after the completion of the reinvestigation.” If the item cannot be verified, 1681i(a)(5)(A) requires the bureau to “promptly delete that item of information from the file of the consumer, or modify that item of information, as appropriate.” Our step-by-step dispute guide covers letter construction.

Step 5. Dispute with the lender as well, not just the bureau

The FTC says to “Dispute it with the business that supplied the information.” 15 U.S.C. 1681s-2(b)(1) puts independent duties on the lender: it must “conduct an investigation with respect to the disputed information” and, if the item is incomplete, inaccurate or unverifiable, modify it, delete it, or permanently block it. Under 1681s-2(a)(1)(A) it may not report information it “knows or has reasonable cause to believe” is inaccurate.

A bureau dispute alone lets the lender click yes in an automated system. A parallel dispute creates a record it must answer.

Step 6. Make a collector validate the deficiency before you pay anything

If the deficiency was sold or assigned, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act applies. Under 15 U.S.C. 1692g(a) the collector must state the amount of the debt and the name of the creditor within five days of first contact. Under 1692g(b), if you dispute in writing within 30 days, “the debt collector shall cease collection of the debt, or any disputed portion thereof, until the debt collector obtains verification of the debt” and mails it to you.

While that runs, 1692c(a)(1) presumes calls before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. local time are inconvenient, 1692c(c) lets you stop contact by written notice, and Regulation F, 12 CFR 1006.14(b)(2)(ii), presumes a violation above seven calls in seven consecutive days on one debt.

One honest limit: 1692a(6)(A) excludes “any officer or employee of a creditor while, in the name of the creditor, collecting debts for such creditor.” If your original lender still collects its own deficiency in its own name, the FDCPA generally does not reach it, though the FCRA and state law still do. See how long collections stay on credit for the reporting clock.

Step 7. Optional: settle the deficiency, and get the terms in writing first

Settling a deficiency does not delete the repossession entry. It zeroes the balance and stops the account aging into a fresh collection placement. Get the terms and the reporting language in writing before any money moves. See pay in full versus settle.

Step 8. Restart on-time payment history immediately

FICO publishes the weights on What’s in my FICO Scores?: “payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of credit history (15%), new credit (10%) and credit mix (10%).” Payment history is the largest category and the one the repossession damaged. FICO adds the caveat that kills every guarantee here: those weights “are for the general population and may be different for different credit profiles.”

Step 9. Bring revolving utilization down

Amounts owed is 30 percent of a FICO Score. With the auto tradeline destroyed, revolving balances carry the score, and paying cards down is the fastest lever you control.

Step 10. Optional: add a secured card or a credit-builder account

A repossession does not stop you opening new positive tradelines. A secured card reporting to all three bureaus rebuilds payment history from month one. Our best secured credit cards comparison covers deposit sizes and bureau coverage.

Step 11. Optional: file a CFPB complaint if the bureau rubber-stamps it

If the investigation returns verified and your documents say otherwise, file at consumerfinance.gov/complaint. Of the approximately 5,806,800 credit or consumer reporting complaints the CFPB received in 2025, companies responded to 99.9 percent and closed 40 percent with non-monetary relief, the category that includes correcting or deleting a tradeline, 51 percent with an explanation and 0.02 percent with monetary relief. Expect to wait: the bureaus get 60 days, and all three recently averaged more than 50. See how to file a CFPB complaint.

What you can dispute and what you cannot

Disputable on a repossession: a wrong first-delinquency date, a voluntary surrender reported as involuntary, a balance that ignores the sale proceeds, the same deficiency listed twice by the lender and a collector, an account that is not yours, a payment grid with lates in the wrong months.

Not disputable: an accurate repossession, inside the seven-year window, on an account you opened. No company, including this one, can lawfully remove it. Anyone promising to is describing something the FCRA does not allow.

How long does recovery actually take?

There is no honest single number for recovery after a repossession, and FICO says so: “it’s difficult to say exactly how much any individual item will decrease your FICO Scores.”

What can be said honestly is the mechanism. Deleting an inaccurate item under 1681i(a)(5)(A) removes its scoring weight at that bureau immediately. Rebuilding is arithmetic on the 35 percent payment history and 30 percent amounts owed categories, and it moves in months, not days.

For someone who disputes the errors, resolves the deficiency, then pays everything on time, a 30 to 60 point move over six to twelve months is a reasonable expectation for many profiles. It is not a promise, and no one may guarantee it. The repossession stays until its seven years expire. What changes before then is what you build on top of it. The short version

  • Seven years under 15 U.S.C. 1681c(a)(5), counted from the first missed payment, not the tow date.
  • An accurate repossession cannot be removed early by anyone. The inaccurate details around it can.
  • The deficiency is a separate debt. In CFPB 2018 to 2022 data, 94 percent of disposals left one, averaging $11,340 by December 2022.
  • Voluntary surrender still reports as a repossession and still leaves you owing the deficiency.
  • Dispute in writing with all three bureaus and with the lender, certified mail. The bureau has 30 days, plus 15, and must delete what it cannot verify.
  • Rebuilding runs on payment history (35 percent) and amounts owed (30 percent). A 30 to 60 point move over six to twelve months is realistic, never guaranteed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a repossession stay on your credit report?

A repossession stays on your credit report for seven years. The Fair Credit Reporting Act, 15 U.S.C. 1681c(a)(5), excludes any adverse item that antedates the report by more than seven years, and 1681c(c)(1) starts that clock 180 days after the first delinquency that led to the repossession. In practice the bureaus and FICO both describe it as seven years from your first missed payment, not from the tow date or the auction date.

Can a repossession be removed from your credit report?

An accurate repossession cannot be removed before the seven-year period ends, and no company can lawfully promise otherwise. An inaccurate one can. Under 15 U.S.C. 1681i(a)(5)(A), a credit bureau must delete or modify any disputed item it cannot verify. The winning disputes are factual: a wrong first-delinquency date, a balance that ignores the auction proceeds, a deficiency reported twice, or an account that is not yours.

What is a deficiency balance after a repossession?

The FTC defines a deficiency as the difference between what you owe on your contract, plus certain expenses, and what your lender gets for selling the car. The CFPB gives this example: owe $10,000, the lender sells for $7,500, and you owe $2,500 plus repossession fees. In the CFPB's January 2025 auto finance dataset, 94 percent of the 905,000 vehicle disposals ended with a deficiency balance.

Is voluntary surrender better than a repossession?

Only slightly, and not in the way most people hope. The FTC states that even if you return the car voluntarily you still owe the difference between the contract balance and the sale price. The CFPB counts voluntary surrenders inside its repossession data. Experian says a voluntary repossession is noted on your report and stays seven years. FICO says surrendering voluntarily may reduce the damage, even if only a little.

How long does a debt collector have to validate a deficiency balance?

You have 30 days. Under 15 U.S.C. 1692g(b), if you dispute the debt in writing within 30 days of the collector's first notice, the collector must stop collecting until it mails you verification of the debt or a copy of a judgment. Send it by certified mail with return receipt. Separately, Regulation F at 12 CFR 1006.14(b)(2) presumes a violation when a collector calls more than seven times in seven consecutive days.

How much does a repo hurt my credit score?

There is no honest point figure, and FICO says so directly: "it's difficult to say exactly how much any individual item will decrease your FICO Scores." The damage is also not one entry. FICO lists the late payments, the loan default as a separate event, the repossession of the collateral, and a collection account on the unpaid deficiency. Payment history is the most influential factor in a FICO Score at 35 percent, which is why the run of missed payments before the tow often costs more than the repossession line itself. Anyone quoting you an exact point drop is guessing.

Can I get a new car loan after repossession?

Usually yes, but on worse terms, and lenders do originate well below the median. In the Federal Reserve Bank of New York's 2026 Q2 Household Debt and Credit data, the median credit score at auto loan origination was 716, while the 25th percentile was 639 and the 10th percentile was 580. So roughly one in ten new auto loans went to a borrower scoring at or below 580. Expect a higher rate and a larger down payment. Rebuilding on-time payment history first is what moves the terms.
