How Fast Can Fraudulent Accounts Be Removed?

The legal step takes 4 business days. Everything else is logistics. Here is the realistic day-by-day timeline from discovery to a clean report.

Alexander Katsman

September 2, 2026•11 min read

Fraudulent accounts can be blocked from a credit report in 4 business days. That is the statutory deadline in Section 605B of the Fair Credit Reporting Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2. Everything that stretches a case into months is document gathering, mail transit, and how fast the consumer reacts.

The Federal Trade Commission recorded 1,135,291 identity theft reports in 2024, which is 17.5% of all 6,471,708 reports filed with its Consumer Sentinel Network that year. Credit card identity theft was the largest single type at 449,032 reports, of which 406,110 involved a brand new account opened in someone else’s name, up 7% from 2023. New fraudulent accounts are exactly what Section 605B was written to remove.

The one deadline that matters

FCRA Section 605B, codified at 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2(a), requires a credit bureau to block information resulting from identity theft “not later than 4 business days after the date of receipt” of four specific items:

  1. Appropriate proof of the identity of the consumer
  2. A copy of an identity theft report
  3. The identification of such information by the consumer
  4. A statement by the consumer that the information is not information relating to any transaction by the consumer

Four business days. Not 30. Not “a reasonable time.” For comparison, an ordinary accuracy reinvestigation under FCRA Section 611, 15 U.S.C. 1681i(a)(1)(A), gives the credit bureau 30 days, extendable to 45 days under 1681i(a)(1)(B) if the consumer sends relevant additional information during that first 30 day window.

The catch sits in the statutory word receipt. The 4 business day clock does not start when you file at IdentityTheft.gov or drop the envelope in the box. It starts when a complete package containing all four items lands at the credit bureau. That single fact explains almost every slow identity theft case.

The realistic timeline

For a straightforward identity theft case, 3 to 5 weeks from discovery to updated credit reports is realistic. The statutory block under FCRA Section 605B accounts for only 4 business days of that total. The remaining 20 or so days are document assembly, certified mail transit, and bureau file processing, all of which sit outside the statute.

Where the time is actually lost

Most of the delay in removing a fraudulent account comes from the consumer’s own paperwork, not from a stalling credit bureau. In its December 2025 report under FCRA Section 611(e)(5), the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau found the share of complaining consumers reporting a previous dispute “has hovered around 90% since 2020 for all three” nationwide credit bureaus, while Equifax’s own confirmation rate “hovered around 37% from 2024 through mid-2025” and TransUnion stayed near 30%. A paper trail the bureau does not acknowledge is worth the same as none at all.

Sending an incomplete package

A Section 605B package missing any of the four items listed in 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2(a) is not a slow package. It is a package that never started the 4 business day clock, and it is the most common failure by a wide margin.

The usual gaps in a 605B identity theft block package:

  • Proof of address that does not match. If you moved recently, send proof for both the old and the new address
  • Accounts not identified specifically. “All fraudulent accounts” is not identification under 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2(a)(3). Give creditor name, account number as shown, date opened and amount, per item
  • No signed statement. The sworn sentence required by 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2(a)(4) is a separate required item, not something implied by the identity theft report

Using an ordinary dispute instead of a block

Sending an ordinary accuracy dispute instead of a Section 605B identity theft block is the single most expensive wrong turn, because it swaps a 4 business day deadline for a 30 day one, plus 5 business days after the reinvestigation ends to send written results under 15 U.S.C. 1681i(a)(6)(A).

The structural difference matters more than the calendar. A Section 611 reinvestigation asks the furnisher a question and accepts its answer. A Section 605B block under 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2 issues an instruction to the credit bureau. For identity theft, use the block.

No proof of delivery

Certified mail with return receipt is what converts “I sent it” into a legally provable receipt date, and the receipt date is the exact date the 4 business day deadline in 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2(a) starts running. The Federal Trade Commission tells consumers to “send your letter by certified mail and pay for a ‘return receipt’ so you have a record the credit bureau got it.”

Not escalating

If the 4 business days pass and nothing happens, escalate the same week. Waiting three more weeks “to be polite” is the most expensive thing you can do. A CFPB complaint is free, and the CFPB states that “companies generally respond in 15 days” and that “in some cases, the company will let you know their response is in progress and provide a final response in 60 days.”

Set expectations honestly for the three nationwide bureaus specifically. The CFPB’s December 2025 FCRA Section 611(e)(5) report found that the bureaus “have 60 days to respond to consumers’ complaints” and that “in the most recent months, all three NCRAs have average response times that exceed 50 days.”

The route still works. In its 2025 Consumer Response Annual Report, published March 2026, the CFPB reported approximately 5,806,800 credit or consumer reporting complaints in 2025, which is 88% of all complaints it received, and that companies responded to 99.9% of those sent to them, closing 40% with non-monetary relief, 51% with an explanation, and 0.02% with monetary relief.

What blocking does and does not do

A Section 605B block removes the fraudulent accounts from your credit file and stops the furnisher from reporting them again. It does not create good credit.

Once a credit bureau blocks information under 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2(a), subsection (b) requires it to “promptly notify the furnisher” that the information may be a result of identity theft, that a block has been requested, and of the effective dates of the block. Separately, 15 U.S.C. 1681s-2(a)(6)(B) provides that once a consumer submits an identity theft report to a furnisher at its designated address, that furnisher “may not furnish such information that purports to relate to the consumer to any consumer reporting agency, unless the person subsequently knows or is informed by the consumer that the information is correct.”

A block is not permanent by right. Under 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2(c), a credit bureau may decline or rescind a block if it reasonably determines the block was made in error, was based on a material misrepresentation by the consumer, or that the consumer obtained goods, services or money from the transaction.

What blocking cannot do is manufacture a score. We cannot tell you how many points removing fraudulent accounts will move your credit score, and nobody honestly can. It depends on how much of your file was fraudulent, which scoring model a lender pulls, and what your real history looks like underneath. Accurate negative information that genuinely belongs to you is not removable by a 605B block, because it is not identity theft.

What is measurable is which factors carry the most weight. FICO states that payment history is “approximately 35%” and amounts owed “approximately 30%” of the score calculation, so removing fraudulent late payments and fraudulent balances addresses the two largest components directly.

Complications that extend it

Three complications reliably push a Section 605B timeline past 5 weeks: fraud spread across several account types, a furnisher that keeps reporting after the block, and a package the credit bureau rules incomplete. The Federal Trade Commission found that in 2024, 14% of identity theft reports included more than one type of identity theft, so multi-front cases are the norm.

Loan and lease fraud sprawls most. The FTC recorded 176,400 loan or lease identity theft reports in 2024. A single report can name more than one subtype, so the subtype counts below do not add up to that total.

The other complications, and what each costs you:

  • A furnisher that keeps reporting. After a 605B block the bureau must notify the furnisher under 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2(b), and 15 U.S.C. 1681s-2(a)(6)(B) bars that furnisher from re-reporting. If it reappears, that is a separate and more serious violation. See when a creditor will not remove a fraudulent account
  • Accounts in collections. A debt collector is a separate company with separate FCRA duties. Send it the FTC Identity Theft Report as well
  • A bureau ruling the package incomplete. Ask exactly what was missing, fix exactly that, resend certified. Do not rebuild from scratch
  • Fraud that is old. Accounts opened years ago are harder to document, but 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2 sets no time limit on the block

Credit bureaus and information furnishers were the most complained-about category in the FTC’s 2024 data, with 1,353,175 reports, the top entry among all “Other” categories that year. Slow handling is the norm, not bad luck, which is why escalating on schedule matters.

How to compress it

The fastest identity theft recoveries do five things, and all five happen in the first 48 hours.

  1. Pull all three credit reports before filing anything. Specific identification is a required item under 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2(a)(3). The FTC confirms all three nationwide bureaus “have permanently extended a program that lets you check your credit report from each once a week for free at AnnualCreditReport.com”
  2. File at IdentityTheft.gov the same day. The FTC Identity Theft Report is required item 2 under 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2(a), so nothing else can start without it
  3. Place the fraud alert or freeze immediately. An initial fraud alert lasts 1 year and an extended alert 7 years under 15 U.S.C. 1681c-1. A security freeze must be placed within 1 business day of a toll free telephone or secure electronic request under 15 U.S.C. 1681c-1(i)(2)(A), and lifted within 1 hour under 1681c-1(i)(3)(C). The FTC confirms there is “no cost to place or lift a credit freeze”
  4. Do not wait for a police report to mail your 605B packages. You generally do not need one for the bureaus. Get it in parallel if you need it for an extended fraud alert. See Identity Theft Report vs police report
  5. Certified with return receipt, and diary day 4 and day 10. The receipt proves the date the 4 business day clock started. Escalate on schedule rather than on feeling

After the fraud is cleared

Once the fraudulent accounts are blocked, what remains is your real credit file, and rebuilding it runs on a far slower clock than the 4 business day block. No paperwork step substitutes for time and payment behavior.

For context on where a rebuilt file is heading, FICO reported the national average FICO Score at 715 as of February 2025, down one point from January 2025 and two points from April 2024, and average credit card utilization at 36.1% in February 2025, above the pre pandemic level of 35.0% in January 2020. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York reported that as of the end of June 2026, 4.7% of all outstanding household debt was in some stage of delinquency, against a total household debt balance of $18.77 trillion. A clean file is worth more in that environment, not less.

The short version

  • The legal step for removing a fraudulent account is 4 business days under 15 U.S.C. 1681c-2, not 30
  • An ordinary accuracy reinvestigation under 15 U.S.C. 1681i is 30 days, extendable to 45, which is why the block is the right tool for identity theft
  • The clock starts when a complete package is received, not when it is sent
  • Realistic end to end for a clean case: 3 to 5 weeks
  • Certified mail with return receipt, always, because the receipt sets the deadline
  • Escalate to the CFPB on schedule. The three nationwide bureaus have 60 days and recently averaged more than 50
  • Blocking restores the truth in your file. It does not manufacture a score

Frequently Asked Questions

How fast can fraudulent accounts legally be removed?

The block itself is 4 business days. FCRA Section 605B requires a credit bureau to block information resulting from identity theft no later than 4 business days after it receives proof of your identity, a copy of an identity theft report, your identification of the information, and your statement that it is not yours. The rest of the timeline is paperwork and mail, not law.

Can I really clear fraud from my credit in 30 days?

For a straightforward case where the accounts are clearly fraudulent and you send complete packages promptly, 3 to 5 weeks from discovery to updated reports is realistic. Complications extend it: a bureau ruling a package incomplete, a furnisher that keeps reporting, or fraud spread across many creditors.

Does removing fraudulent accounts raise my credit score?

It removes their effect, which can be significant if the fraudulent accounts carried missed payments or large balances. How much your score moves depends entirely on what is left underneath. We cannot promise a specific number of points, and anyone who does is not being straight with you.

Why do some people take six months?

Almost always one of four things: they used ordinary dispute letters instead of a 605B block, their package was incomplete so the clock never started, they had no proof of delivery when the bureau said nothing arrived, or a furnisher kept re-reporting and they did not escalate.

What is the single biggest thing that speeds this up?

Sending a complete package the first time. The 4 business day clock only starts when all four required items arrive. Most delays are not the bureau stalling, they are a package that was missing proof of address or did not identify accounts specifically enough.
